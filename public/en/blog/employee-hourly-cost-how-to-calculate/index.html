What does an hour of work really cost you? How to calculate an employee's hourly cost

10 November 2026 · 3 min read · Tymex

In short

  • Hourly cost is not salary divided by hours: contributions, accruals and overheads all count.
  • It must be divided by the hours actually worked, not the theoretical hours in the contract.
  • It is the starting point for every quote and for calculating job margins.

Why hourly cost is the most important number

Every quote, every job, every margin calculation starts from one question: how much does an hour of this person's work cost the company?

Get that wrong and everything after it is wrong too. An underestimated hourly cost makes loss-making jobs look profitable.

What goes into the cost of an employee

Gross pay is only the most visible part. The annual cost to the company usually includes:

  • annual gross pay, including any extra monthly payments set by the contract
  • employer social security contributions
  • severance pay accruals (in Italy, TFR)
  • overheads attributable to the person: training, equipment, company car, phone, meal vouchers

Contribution and accrual rates depend on the collective agreement and the job grade: your payroll adviser can give you the exact figures.

The hours that really count

The second typical mistake is dividing by theoretical hours. A 40-hour week makes 2,080 hours a year on paper, but holidays, public holidays, leave and an estimate for sick days have to come off.

Then there's one more step: not all hours worked are productive. Internal meetings, travel, training and admin are necessary, but they can't be charged to a job.

A step-by-step example

Here's an example with hypothetical figures, just to show the method.

Annual cost to the company:

  • gross pay: €30,000
  • contributions and severance accrual (assumed for the example): €11,000
  • overheads: €3,000

Total: €44,000.

Annual hours:

  • theoretical hours: 40 hours × 52 weeks = 2,080 hours
  • less holidays, public holidays and leave (assumed for the example): 300 hours

Hours actually worked: 1,780.

Hourly cost = €44,000 ÷ 1,780 hours = €24.72 per hour

If productive hours, the ones that can be charged to jobs, are 75% of the total, useful hours drop to 1,335. The cost per productive hour rises to €32.96.

That's the number to use when you prepare a quote: non-productive hours still have to be paid, and something has to cover them.

The most common mistakes

  • using net pay, or gross pay alone
  • dividing by the theoretical hours in the contract
  • forgetting overheads
  • using the same hourly cost for everyone regardless of role
  • not updating it after contract renewals or pay rises

From hourly cost to job cost

Once you know each person's hourly cost, the labour cost of a job is the hours logged multiplied by the hourly cost of whoever worked them. That takes two ingredients: correct hourly costs and accurately logged hours.

In short

The right hourly cost is calculated on the full cost to the company, divided by the hours actually worked, or better still the productive ones. Recalculate it at least once a year: it is the basis of every reliable quote.
